# A History of Quantum Mechanics — and How It Became a Computer (Beginner)

## In Plain English

Every story about quantum computing actually starts as a story about a German physicist trying to save his career.

In 1900, Max Planck was staring at a problem that had embarrassed physics for years: nobody could correctly predict the color of light glowing off a hot object (the "ultraviolet catastrophe," a genuinely alarming name for what was, at the time, a genuinely alarming failure of classical physics — the equations predicted infinite energy at high frequencies, which is obviously nonsense). Planck found a fix, but it required assuming something bizarre: that energy isn't smooth and continuous, like water pouring from a tap, but comes in discrete little packets — "quanta." Planck himself didn't believe this was physically real; he treated it as a mathematical trick to make the numbers work, something he'd later call "an act of despair." He was wrong to doubt it. It was the first crack in three hundred years of classical physics, and neither he nor anyone else at the time had any idea it would, a century later, spawn a whole new kind of computer.

Five years on, a young patent clerk named Albert Einstein took Planck's "trick" and insisted it was real — light itself, he argued in 1905, actually travels in these discrete packets (later named "photons"), which is why shining light on a metal plate knocks electrons loose in a very particular, quantized way (the photoelectric effect — the very paper that, ironically, won Einstein his Nobel Prize, not relativity). Then, in 1913, Niels Bohr used the same "energy comes in steps" idea to explain why atoms don't collapse and why they glow in specific colors rather than a smooth rainbow — electrons, he proposed, can only occupy certain fixed orbits, like rungs on a ladder, never the space between rungs.

By the mid-1920s, a remarkable, fast-moving cluster of mostly very young physicists — Werner Heisenberg (23 years old), Erwin Schrödinger, Max Born, Wolfgang Pauli, Paul Dirac — turned these scattered clues into a full mathematical theory: quantum mechanics. Heisenberg's 1925 version used strange grids of numbers (matrices) that famously didn't commute — multiplying A by B gave a different answer than B by A, a genuinely shocking violation of ordinary arithmetic intuition that turned out to be the mathematical seed of the uncertainty principle. Schrödinger, unaware of Heisenberg's work, published a completely different-looking wave-based version just months later in 1926 — and then, in one of physics' tidier surprises, it was shown the two apparently unrelated formulations were secretly the same theory in different clothes.

This new theory came with a philosophical bombshell that split its own founders. Bohr argued the universe is genuinely, fundamentally probabilistic at its core — not "probabilistic because we lack information," but probabilistic in some deeper, irreducible sense. Einstein never accepted this, spending the rest of his life arguing quantum mechanics had to be an incomplete description of a deeper, deterministic reality — captured in his famous line, "God does not play dice with the universe." Bohr's now-legendary reply: "Einstein, stop telling God what to do." Their argument (the Bohr-Einstein debates, running roughly 1927 to Einstein's death in 1955) produced, almost as a side effect, the 1935 EPR paper — Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen's attempt to prove quantum mechanics was broken, which instead accidentally gave the world its first rigorous description of entanglement (see `01-beginner/02-superposition-and-entanglement.md`). It took until 1964 for physicist John Bell to figure out how to actually test who was right, and decades more of increasingly precise experiments (earning Alain Aspect, John Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger the 2022 Nobel Prize) to settle it: Bohr was right, and the universe really is that strange.

For roughly the next four decades, quantum mechanics was spectacularly successful physics — it explained chemistry, built the transistor, powered lasers, MRI machines, and the entire modern electronics industry — but nobody thought of it as a form of *computing*. That leap took one more genius, and one more offhand remark. In 1981, Richard Feynman gave a keynote at MIT pointing out something that should have seemed obvious in hindsight but had escaped everyone: simulating a quantum system on an ordinary computer requires resources that grow exponentially with the system's size (exactly the wall described in `02-intermediate/01-multi-qubit-systems.md`), which meant nature itself was doing something no classical computer could efficiently copy. "Nature isn't classical, dammit," he told the room, "and if you want to make a simulation of nature, you'd better make it quantum mechanical." A few years later, in 1985, physicist David Deutsch formalized what a "quantum computer" would even mean mathematically, describing a universal quantum Turing machine. For a decade afterward, this stayed a niche academic curiosity — until 1994, when Peter Shor showed a quantum computer could factor large numbers exponentially faster than any known classical method, instantly making the entire idea a matter of national-security and industry-funding urgency, because factoring difficulty is what protects most of the world's encrypted communication. Lov Grover's 1996 search algorithm followed, and the modern field of quantum computing — the one the rest of this repo is about — was properly born.

## Two Experiments That Made It Undeniable

Theories are one thing; quantum mechanics became impossible to dismiss because of a handful of experiments so strange that no classical explanation could touch them.

**The double-slit experiment.** Fire particles — electrons, or single photons of light — one at a time, at a screen with two narrow slits cut in it, and record where each one lands on a detector behind it. Common sense says each particle goes through one slit or the other, landing in one of two clusters. That's not what happens. Instead, over many particles, an interference pattern builds up — stripes of high and low probability, exactly like two overlapping water ripples reinforcing and cancelling each other. Somehow, even sent through one at a time, each particle behaves as if it passed through *both* slits at once and interfered with itself. This single experiment is often called the clearest demonstration of quantum weirdness there is — physicist Richard Feynman called it a phenomenon "impossible, absolutely impossible, to explain in any classical way," and said it contains "the only mystery" of quantum mechanics.

**Heisenberg's microscope.** In 1927, Werner Heisenberg offered a now-famous thought experiment to make his uncertainty principle (see the math section below) feel physically intuitive rather than just an abstract formula. To see an electron's exact position, you'd need to bounce light off it — but light of a short enough wavelength to pin down a precise position necessarily carries enough energy to give the electron's momentum a real, unavoidable kick, blurring out exactly what you were trying to also measure. Use gentler light instead, and the electron's momentum stays undisturbed — but now the light's longer wavelength can't resolve its position precisely. Push measurement precision on one side, and you unavoidably lose it on the other — not because of a clumsy microscope, but because that's the deal nature offers.

## How Qubits Are Actually Built Today

For the theory's first century, "quantum weirdness" mostly lived in thought experiments and delicate physics-lab demonstrations. Turning it into a *computer* meant answering a brutally practical engineering question nobody in the 1920s had to think about: how do you build a real, physical, controllable two-level system, keep it isolated enough from the world to stay coherent, and still be able to reach in and flip it on command?

The first-ever single, controllable qubit was trapped in 1995 by Chris Monroe and David Wineland at NIST — a single charged beryllium ion, held in place by electromagnetic fields and manipulated with lasers (Wineland later shared the 2012 Nobel Prize for this work). That was the proof of principle. Since then, the race to build better qubits has splintered into several genuinely different physical approaches, each betting on a different piece of real-world matter to hold quantum information:

- **Superconducting circuits** (IBM, Google, Rigetti) — tiny loops of metal cooled to near absolute zero, so cold that electrical resistance vanishes and the loop behaves like a single, controllable "artificial atom." This is the approach behind most of the qubit-count headlines you've probably seen.
- **Trapped ions** (IonQ, Quantinuum) — individual charged atoms suspended in a vacuum by electromagnetic fields, controlled with precisely tuned lasers — a direct descendant of that original 1995 NIST demonstration, just scaled up.
- **Neutral atoms** (QuEra, Pasqal, Atom Computing) — individual atoms held in place by focused laser "tweezers," a newer approach that's scaled qubit counts unusually fast.
- **Topological qubits** (Microsoft) — a more speculative bet on exotic quasiparticles that would be inherently more error-resistant, if the underlying physics holds up.
- **Photonic qubits** (PsiQuantum, Xanadu) — quantum information carried by individual particles of light itself, closing a strange loop back to Einstein's 1905 photon.

Every one of these is, underneath the engineering, still just a physical system built to have exactly two clean, controllable energy levels — the same "rungs on a ladder" idea Bohr proposed for the atom back in 1913, just re-engineered a century later, purpose-built, and cooled, trapped, or laser-controlled into submission. `qoherence-hardware/docs` covers each of these platforms in full technical depth — this page is the hundred-year running start; that module is where the story gets specific about the actual hardware built to run the ideas coming up next.

## Now With the Math — a first, gentle look

You'll see far more of this notation built up carefully, piece by piece, over the next several chapters — this section is just a preview, so the symbols feel like old friends rather than strangers when they return.

**Planck's relation, `E = hf`.** The energy `E` of one quantum of light is proportional to its frequency `f` (how fast it oscillates), with `h` — Planck's constant, an extremely small fixed number (`≈ 6.626×10⁻³⁴` in standard units) — as the proportionality factor. This one small equation is where "energy comes in discrete packets" first became precise, testable science rather than philosophy.

**Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, `Δx · Δp ≥ ħ/2`.** `Δx` is how uncertain you are about a particle's position, `Δp` is how uncertain you are about its momentum, and `ħ` ("h-bar") is Planck's constant divided by `2π` — a bookkeeping convenience that shows up constantly in quantum formulas. The inequality says the product of these two uncertainties can never shrink below a fixed floor — pin down position precisely and momentum necessarily gets blurrier, and vice versa. This isn't a statement about clumsy measurement equipment; it's a hard limit built into nature itself, and it's the same fundamental "can't know everything precisely at once" character that later reappears, transformed, as the reason a qubit's superposition is irreducibly probabilistic (`01-beginner/01-what-is-a-qubit.md`).

**The Schrödinger equation, `iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Hψ`.** This is the master equation governing how a quantum state `ψ` (the same `ψ` you'll meet again as `|ψ⟩` in the very next chapter) changes over time. `i` is the imaginary unit (`√-1`), `∂ψ/∂t` means "the rate of change of `ψ` with respect to time," and `H` (the Hamiltonian) is a matrix encoding the system's energy — the same Hamiltonian that reappears in `qoherence-algorithms/docs/03-advanced/01-vqe-and-qaoa-theory.md` as the very thing VQE is built to find the lowest eigenvalue of. The crucial fact worth carrying forward from this equation, without needing to solve it: it's *linear*, and that single mathematical property is the deep reason superposition is allowed to exist at all — a point `qoherence-core/docs/02-intermediate/02-linear-algebra-foundations.md` returns to and explains in full.
