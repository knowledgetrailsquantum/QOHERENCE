# Industry Case Studies: Where Quantum AI Meets Real Business

## In Plain English

This page walks through how quantum and quantum-inspired AI are actually being tried in real industries today — not as thought experiments, but as pilots, partnerships, and (in a few cases) production systems. The pattern across nearly every sector is the same: quantum-*inspired* classical techniques (annealing, tensor networks) are delivering real value today, while true quantum-hardware QML remains mostly in the pilot/research stage, limited by today's small, noisy devices.

**Healthcare & drug discovery.** Molecules are natively quantum systems, so simulating them is a natural fit for quantum computers long-term (see `qoherence-docs/docs/applications-by-industry.md`). Today, most near-term healthcare value actually comes from quantum-inspired optimization — for example, drug-candidate scheduling and clinical trial optimization run on quantum-inspired annealers, while true quantum simulation of larger molecules awaits bigger, less noisy hardware.

**Fintech.** Portfolio optimization (finding the best mix of investments under constraints) is a natural fit for annealing-style optimization. Banks including Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan Chase, and HSBC have run public pilots using both real quantum annealers (D-Wave) and quantum-inspired chips (Fujitsu Digital Annealer) for portfolio optimization and fraud-pattern detection, generally reporting competitive-but-not-yet-dominant results versus tuned classical methods — an honest, in-progress state rather than a finished win.

**Logistics & supply chain.** This is arguably the sector with the most mature quantum-inspired deployments today. Vehicle routing (deciding delivery-truck routes to minimize total distance/time) and warehouse-slotting problems map naturally onto the "landscape search" framing from `02-intermediate/03-quantum-inspired-optimization.md`. Volkswagen ran a widely-reported 2019 pilot routing Lisbon's public buses using D-Wave's quantum annealer, and DHL has publicly discussed quantum-inspired route optimization pilots. These remain pilots or narrow deployments rather than wholesale replacements of existing classical logistics software, but the direction of travel — quantum-inspired annealing chips handling well-defined optimization sub-problems inside a larger classical logistics pipeline — is a genuinely useful and growing pattern.

**Cybersecurity.** Two very different threads: (1) quantum computing as a *threat* to today's encryption (already covered in `qoherence-docs/docs/ethics-and-society.md`'s "harvest now, decrypt later" discussion), and (2) QML explored as a *defense* tool — pattern-detection for network intrusion or malware classification, an active but early research area with no broad production deployment yet.

**Manufacturing & robotics.** Quantum-inspired optimization is being explored for production scheduling and robotic-path planning — the same underlying "search a huge landscape for the best configuration" problem shape as logistics routing, applied to factory floors and robot arms instead of delivery trucks.

**NLP.** Genuinely quantum approaches to language processing remain mostly academic (small-scale, proof-of-concept demonstrations) as of 2026 — today's large language models are entirely classical, and no credible near-term quantum path competes with them at scale; this is one of the clearer places where quantum-AI hype has outpaced quantum-AI reality.

**A concrete real-world hook: quick commerce.** Think about a 10-minute grocery-delivery app deciding, in real time, which of a dozen nearby riders should take which order, on which route, so nothing melts and nothing is late. That's a live vehicle-load and routing optimization problem repeated thousands of times a day, and it's exactly the kind of "quick commerce" case researchers point to as a natural early home for quantum-inspired logistics optimization — the same landscape-search framing used for slower delivery networks, just running on a much tighter clock (a theme covered under "quantum leap in quick commerce" logistics research).

**Named case-study threads worth knowing.** A growing body of published research organizes quantum-AI-in-logistics work into recognizable buckets rather than one blob: vehicle load optimization for smart logistics (packing and routing trucks so capacity isn't wasted); quantum-enhanced customer retention, where predictive analytics try to spot which customers are about to churn and route retention offers accordingly; personalized digital marketing, aiming at "hyper-personalized" engagement by crunching customer-behavior data faster; predictive analytics for consumer electronics demand forecasting; and quantum-blockchain approaches to fraud detection, pairing quantum-flavored anomaly detection with blockchain's tamper-evident record-keeping for supply-chain risk management. None of these are single silver-bullet products — they're research directions, each with its own open questions about whether the "quantum" part is pulling real weight yet.

**Industry 4.0 and Industry 5.0 framing.** A useful way to place all of this: Industry 4.0 was about connecting factories and supply chains digitally (sensors, data, automation); Industry 5.0 adds a human-centric layer on top — technology in service of resilience, sustainability, and people, not just throughput. Quantum machine learning research increasingly gets framed as an Industry 4.0/5.0 tool: helping human decision-makers handle problems (scheduling, risk, personalization) that are too combinatorially large for classical software to search exhaustively, while keeping people in the loop rather than replacing them.

## Now With the Math

Across these cases, the recurring pattern is: real-world business problems (routing, scheduling, portfolio allocation, molecule scoring) get reformulated as **QUBO** (Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization) problems — a standard mathematical form where you're choosing binary (yes/no) variables to minimize a cost function built from pairs of those variables. QUBO is the shared input format both real quantum annealers (D-Wave) and quantum-inspired chips (Fujitsu, Toshiba) are built to consume, which is why so many different industries' problems get funneled through the same underlying quantum-inspired-optimization pipeline described in `02-intermediate/03-quantum-inspired-optimization.md`.

## A Bit of History

Volkswagen's 2019 Lisbon bus-routing pilot with D-Wave is one of the most cited "quantum computing meets logistics" case studies precisely because it was public, concrete, and modestly scoped (a handful of bus routes, not a whole city's traffic system) — a good model of how these pilots are typically run: narrow, well-defined sub-problems carved out of a much larger classical system. Similarly, Goldman Sachs' quantum research team, active since 2020, has been notably candid in public talks about which near-term techniques (mostly quantum-inspired and hybrid) show promise versus which remain purely theoretical (deep fault-tolerant algorithms) — a candor this repo tries to model throughout. Zooming out further, industry-transformation researchers place quantum AI on a longer historical timeline of industrial revolutions — mechanization (1.0), electrification and mass production (2.0), computing and automation (3.0), digital connectivity (4.0), and now a human-centric "5.0" phase where technologies like quantum computing and AI are framed as tools in service of people and sustainability rather than throughput alone. Whether quantum AI actually earns a chapter in that story, or turns out to be a smaller footnote within the Industry 4.0/5.0 wave, is still an open question — but the framing is a useful reminder that today's pilots are early moves in a much longer game.
